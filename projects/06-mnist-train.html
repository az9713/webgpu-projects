The science

The network is 784 → 64 → 10: a linear map, a rectifier, a second linear map, and a softmax that turns ten scores into a probability distribution, pi = ezi / Σj ezj. Training minimises the cross-entropy L = −log py for the true class y.

Composing those two gives the same cancellation that logistic regression gives, in ten dimensions at once:

∂L/∂z = p − y, with y the one-hot label.

The full Jacobian of the softmax is a 10×10 matrix, and the gradient of the logarithm is its exact inverse in the direction that matters, so the whole thing collapses to a subtraction. That is why the backward pass here starts with one Sub and no special-cased softmax derivative.

The hidden layer is what a linear model cannot supply. A bare softmax on pixels is a linear classifier and tops out near 92% on MNIST, because the classes are not linearly separable in pixel space — a 7 and a 1 overlap heavily pixel by pixel. Sixty-four rectified units cut the input space into piecewise-linear regions and let the second layer draw a boundary that bends. The page reaches 94.8% on 2,000 images it never trained on.

Then there is the pad, and it teaches the sharpest lesson. MNIST digits were not merely scanned: each was cropped to its ink, scaled so the longer side is 20 pixels, and placed by centre of mass in a 28×28 field. The pad reproduces that pipeline exactly. Skip it and the network sees a distribution it has never met, and misreads a digit that looks perfectly clear to you.

Forward: MatMul, Add, Relu, MatMul, Add, Softmax. The gradient of softmax with cross-entropy is one Sub. Backward: Transpose, MatMul, ReduceSum, Greater, Where, then Mul and Sub for the updates. The loss is Log, Mul, ReduceSum, ReduceMean. Each batch is shuffled and uploaded from JavaScript; the weights never leave the card. The data is the TensorFlow.js MNIST sprite, 10.7 MB, fetched from Google's public bucket: the first 20,000 images train, the next 2,000 test. A linear model reaches about 92%; the hidden layer does better.
